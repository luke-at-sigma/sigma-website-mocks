The argument

BI told you what happened.
AI Apps decide what happens next.

An AI App is software, built for one specific job, that runs directly on your data warehouse. It shows live numbers like a dashboard, but it also lets people change those numbers, kick off a workflow, and put AI to work inside the same security perimeter your warehouse already enforces. That's the shape we'll keep coming back to throughout this playbook.

Business intelligence has been a window for twenty years, and the people on the other side of that window have been quietly losing patience. They look through it, read what's there, then open a spreadsheet and start typing. Most enterprises still operate this way, which leaves them with what is effectively a read-only company: one that can see itself in fine detail but cannot actually move on what it sees.

AI Apps change that because they don't stop at showing the number. Users can edit it right there in the same screen, and that edit writes back to the warehouse with a full audit trail. The same AI that helps build the app reads the live data and surfaces what to do next, so the screen where the team sees the numbers becomes the screen where the team makes decisions and acts on them.

This playbook is a practical guide to that shift. We cover why customers across finance, ops, and product are building AI Apps right now, what the four parts of one actually look like, and how to ship the first one in about four weeks.

Chapter two

What an AI App actually is

The phrase gets thrown around loosely, which is part of why we wrote a definition narrow enough to disagree with. An AI App is built from four parts that have to work together, and the work they do together is what changes what a data tool can be.

Working definition

An AI App is software built for one job, powered by your data platform, that shows live data, writes back to the warehouse, and runs AI inside the same security perimeter your warehouse already enforces.

The four parts

Miss any one of them and what you have is something else: a dashboard, a form, a chatbot, or a script that gets the job almost done. AI Apps need all four working together.

01

Live warehouse data

The app queries the warehouse directly, with no extracts and no nightly copies, so the moment the warehouse updates, the app updates.

02

Data capture in the app

The people closest to the work can capture decisions, overrides, forecasts, and approvals directly in the screen they're already looking at, and every change flows back to the warehouse where it's logged and governed.

03

Governed AI inside the perimeter

AI helps build the app, reads the live data, and powers chat for end users, all of it operating inside the same security boundary the warehouse already enforces, so nothing leaves the perimeter your security team has approved.

04

Action sequences

A click or a change in the data kicks off a sequence that can insert a row, run a stored procedure, send a Slack alert, or fire an API.

From dashboard to AI App

A dashboard does this

Shows the signal

  • Pulls from the warehouse, usually through an extract.
  • Draws charts and tables.
  • Filters when someone clicks.
  • Exports a PDF or CSV when asked.
  • Stops there.
An AI App does all that, plus

Acts on the signal

  • Lets users edit, override, and submit values. The change goes back to the warehouse.
  • Kicks off an approval, a stored procedure, or an API call.
  • Calls Sigma Assistant or a custom Sigma agent. All inside the perimeter.
  • Keeps a full audit trail. Who changed what, and when.
  • Becomes the system the team actually uses to do the work.
Chapter three

What customers are building right now

We pulled the patterns customers described across 2,099 sales calls in the last six months, and five of them came up over and over again. None of these are theoretical exercises in a workshop deck; they show up in finance, operations, product, and engineering conversations every single week.

Pattern 01

Spreadsheet replacement, with write-back

Take an Excel model that has to roll up across sites, funds, or SKUs and turn it into an input table inside an AI App. Edits now flow back into the warehouse where they're governed and audited, the macros disappear, and two people can edit the same data at the same time without colliding. Tariff pricing, loan books, reserves, and demand plans all sit in this pattern.

Customer voice Thirteen siloed Excel spreadsheets across two brands with no central source, running an eight-month tariff cycle for 2,000 SKUs that represent 40 percent of revenue.
Pattern 02

Approval workflows on live data

Someone sees a number that needs to change and proposes the change inside the app, where a manager approves or rejects it through an action sequence. The reviewer, the timestamp, and the new value all land in the warehouse together as a single audit row. This is how KPI scoring, expense overrides, and reserves approvals all run.

Customer voice Manual Google Sheets workflows for KPI scoring are clunky and error-prone, and none of the changes ever write back to Snowflake or Salesforce.
Pattern 03

Embedded customer analytics with AI chat

Sigma embeds inside your customer's product, the warehouse handles tenant isolation through row-level security, and Sigma Assistant answers customer questions in plain English. Customers build their own reports, schedule their own exports, and stop generating ticket queues for your services team.

Customer voice A live AI chatbot with natural language queries, LLM-driven dashboard generation, semantic layers, and multi-tenant AI analytics for client portals.
Pattern 04

Agentic operations

A Sigma agent watches the warehouse, and when something looks off, the app shows it with a suggested action and a button to approve, dismiss, or escalate. This is the shape billing audit takes, along with fraud flagging, claim validation, and the account-360 workflows where the next move comes attached to the alert.

Customer voice AI-driven account summaries, AI-powered rep actioning, anomaly detection agents, and AI briefings. Repeatedly named as the biggest pain point to solve.
Pattern 05

Closed-loop forecasting and planning

Forecasts and scenarios go into input tables, actuals come from the warehouse, and the compare lives in one workbook with versioning and approvals built in. The whole Excel-plus-PowerPoint cycle that breaks every quarter simply goes away, replaced by something that's queryable in SQL the moment a number changes.

Customer voice A three-month pricing cycle today, mostly sequential and idle. With Sigma input tables, the team would get live, finance-ready outputs across acquired entities.

Chapter six

How to scope an AI App

Start with a workflow, not a dashboard, and pick a process that lives in spreadsheets, in tickets, or in someone's head. Answer the five questions below and you have a scope worth opening Sigma for.

01

What decision is being made?

Name the specific call that has to happen, whether it's approving a price, refunding a customer, or dispatching a technician. Real decisions always have verbs attached to them.

02

Who has to act on it?

One person on one screen, ideally walking one path through the data. If three different roles need three different views, you have three AI Apps to build, not one.

03

What data already lives in the warehouse?

Pull the schema and confirm the data is actually there, because if it isn't yet, the AI App isn't the first step. Sigma is not the place to land data the warehouse doesn't own.

04

What needs to write back?

Decisions, overrides, comments, approvals, scenario inputs, and anything else a person types that the warehouse should remember. The list you produce here is the list of input tables you'll build.

05

Where does AI fit?

Sigma Assistant handles build and analyze, Sigma agents handle in-app chat, and warehouse agents like Cortex, Genie, or Q handle retrieval over governed data. Pick one for the first version and add the others later.

+

Stop test

If any of the five answers feels fuzzy, that fuzziness is the scoping work itself. Don't open Sigma until each answer fits on a single line.

Chapter seven

Reference architecture

An AI App is built across three layers, and each layer answers a different question about governance and performance. The diagram below reads from the bottom up because the bottom is where the trust starts.

Layer 1 · Foundation

The data platform

Snowflake, Databricks, BigQuery, or Redshift, holding the source tables, dbt models, and semantic views. Row-level security and write-back schemas are defined here, where the security team has already done its review.

Owns lineage, access, audit.

Layer 2 · Sigma

Workbook, input tables, Assistant, agents, actions

Live queries run against the warehouse, input tables write back to a governed schema, and Sigma Assistant builds and reads inside the warehouse perimeter. Sigma agents handle user-facing chat with whatever context you've given them.

Owns the user surface and the write-back grammar.

Layer 3 · Action

Triggers, sequences, integrations

A click in the workbook or a change in the data kicks off an action sequence, stored procedures run against the warehouse, and APIs fire out to Salesforce, Slack, ServiceNow, or whatever services you run yourself.

Owns the bridge from insight to system of record.

All three layers share one identity, one permission model, and one audit trail, which means no user can touch the AI App without permission the warehouse already granted.

Chapter eight

Five patterns to copy

Each pattern below lists the input tables you'll need, the actions you'll wire up, and where AI earns its keep. The right move is to pick one pattern, build it once, and save it as a template the rest of the team can clone.

Pattern A

Spreadsheet replacement

Inputs. Linked input tables backed by warehouse views, plus empty input tables for any new entries the workflow generates.

Actions. Insert, update, and delete row, and a call to a stored procedure that commits the change to the right schema.

AI. Sigma Assistant in build mode for the analyst, with an optional Sigma agent layered in for end-user lookups.

Use when A workflow lives in Excel today: tariff models, loan books, reserves, demand plans, or asset tagging.
Pattern B

Approval workflows

Inputs. Input tables capturing the proposed change, the reviewer, the status field, and the decision timestamp.

Actions. Conditional sequences keyed to status, plus email and Slack notifications and status transitions wired into the workflow.

AI. Sigma Assistant in analyze mode, surfacing what's actually worth a closer look before the reviewer opens the queue.

Use when A manual review queue exists: KPI scoring, expense overrides, claim validation, or manager-approved adjustments.
Pattern C

Embedded customer analytics with AI chat

Inputs. Tenant-scoped input tables for customer feedback, settings, and any overrides the customer can apply.

Actions. JWT-based row-level security and webhook actions firing back to the host application.

AI. Sigma agents for branded customer chat, with warehouse agents handling the context-rich answers underneath.

Use when You sell analytics to your customers: client portals, self-service reporting, white-label dashboards, or customer-facing AI.
Pattern D

Agentic operations

Inputs. Input tables that capture the human decision on whatever the agent surfaces, including dismissals and escalations.

Actions. Triggered sequences fired when a Sigma agent finds an anomaly, with APIs running out to your systems of record.

AI. Sigma agents with custom instructions and tools, paired with warehouse agents for retrieval over governed data.

Use when A workflow needs the data to come find you: billing audit, fraud flagging, churn intervention, or account 360.
Pattern E

Closed-loop forecasting

Inputs. Input tables for forecasts, drivers, scenarios, and commentary, with linked tables that reach actuals from the warehouse.

Actions. Snapshot stored procedures, compare-to-prior actions, and distribution sequences that get the right output to finance on schedule.

AI. Sigma Assistant for variance analysis, with a warehouse agent layered in for plain-English explanations if the audience needs them.

Use when Finance, sales ops, or supply ops owns the cycle: pricing, FP&A, demand, headcount, or capacity.
Chapter nine

A four-week rollout

Four weeks is a fair cadence for shipping the first AI App, with smaller workflows running faster and heavier governance reviews running a little slower. Use what's below as a planning baseline rather than a fixed contract, and remember that the second AI App on this pattern usually ships in half the time.

Week 1

Scope

Answer the five scoping questions, confirm the warehouse data is there, and produce the list of inputs and actions the workflow will need.

Week 2

Build v1

Connect the data, build the workbook, and wire up the first piece of data capture. Get a working prototype in front of a real user before the week ends.

Week 3

Govern and pilot

Add the write-back schema, wire up the action sequences, layer in the AI surface, and open the app to five or ten pilot users. Measure cycle time and error rate as you go.

Week 4

Ship and template

Promote a tagged version to production with a rollback ready, save the pattern as a template the next builder can clone, and pick the next workflow.

Chapter eleven

Where this leaves you

Twenty years of business intelligence taught us how to see our companies from the outside in, mostly through the window of a dashboard. The next twenty are going to be about doing something with what we see, which is what an AI App actually is when you strip away the marketing language.

AI Apps don't replace the dashboard so much as they put the dashboard inside the workflow itself, where the people who actually run the business can act on the numbers without filing a ticket or starting a spreadsheet round-trip. That's the bet, and it's already paying off for the customers we've quoted across these pages, with cycle times collapsing from weeks to minutes and revenue surfacing that nobody on those teams knew was hiding in the data.

The harder part, and the reason we wrote a playbook instead of a blog post, is that the bet only pays off if someone actually ships something. The four parts have to come together. The five questions have to get specific answers. The four weeks have to be four real weeks of building, not four weeks of meetings about building. Every customer getting real value from an AI App today started with one workflow, one user, and one decision they wanted their team to make better.

Pick the workflow tomorrow morning. Open the playbook back to chapter six when you forget where to start. We'll be here.

How we built this playbook

Methodology

We pulled every recorded sales call from September 2025 through May 2026, all 52,895 of them, and ran them through two AI-extracted datasets that fed the analysis behind this playbook.

AI_GONG_MARKETING_PERSONAS

A Cortex-powered persona evaluation for external call participants, with structured fields pulled directly from the transcripts: current state, desired state, capability mapping, cost of inaction, expected outcomes, requested capabilities, and competitive differentiators. We filtered to high-confidence rows for everything in this playbook.

GONG_CALLS_ENRICHED

Call-level transcripts enriched with sentiment scores and per-feature interest scores covering AI, agents, input tables, workbooks, governance, and multi-tenancy. We aggregated across calls to surface what comes up most often and where it shows up most intensely.

Cross-checks

Every customer outcome was checked against Sigma's approved messaging library and any active reference customer agreements. The quotes presented in this playbook are paraphrased syntheses of high-confidence transcript extractions, and named customer numbers are published with permission.
